Explicit micro-directory admission supplies the operational boundary. Qualifying public raw files and specified linked pages enter through the archive's conventions. Completeness means that an admitted file is not merely a fragment or a corrupted object; it does not mean every proposition inside the file is true. The central practical problem is how to make a body of work durable enough that its maker can leave it alone. Repositories, mirrors, M-DISCs, micro-directories, raw sources, manifests, prompts, maps, and summaries all contribute to that aim. Yet each new explanatory layer can enlarge the unfinished task. A map intended to organize closure may need another map; a summary intended to compress history may become a vast new object; a farewell recording may postpone the farewell. The small status line, bounded release, private interval, and corrected chapter offer more attainable forms of completion than an exhaustive final account of the entire person or universe.

The document ultimately preserves an ongoing negotiation rather than a final victory of archive over time. It records an author trying to keep experiences available without pretending that files contain the entirety of life, to use imaginative confinement without surrendering the possibility of actual movement, and to acknowledge uncertainty without abandoning inquiry. Its most consequential achievements are bounded and concrete: a missing item is supplied, a bad label is corrected, an inventory is dated, a lost recording is marked as reconstruction, a bottle is found, a public state is distinguished from a private one, and a living person remains conceptually larger than any fixed derivative.
